Pl. 01

Straight courses

Era
1900s–1920s capes and shingle-style houses
Where you see it
Whole walls. The plainest run, and the most common one.
What the weather does
Bottom courses cup and split first, where splash and snow sit.
How we match it
We weave new shingles into the old joints, so no seam lines up.

Pl. 02

Staggered butts

Era
1900s–1930s shingle style near the bay
Where you see it
Butts set high and low on purpose, so the wall reads hand-laid.
What the weather does
The long butts catch a nor'easter and work loose first.
How we match it
We keep the high-low rhythm instead of laying a straight line through it.

Pl. 03

Fish-scale

Era
1880s Queen Anne gables
Where you see it
Front gables, towers and the band between the floors.
What the weather does
Rounded butts wear thin and the curves break off one by one.
How we match it
We trace a scale still on the house and cut new ones to its curve and width.
Match this pattern
Pl. 04

Diamond point

Era
1880s–1890s Queen Anne and Stick accents
Where you see it
Gable peaks, often mixed with fish in the same wall.
What the weather does
The points split first and leave a ragged line you see from the street.
How we match it
New points cut to the old angle, primed on all sides before they go up.
Match this pattern
Pl. 05

Hexagon

Era
1880s–1900s gables and porch skirts
Where you see it
Porch skirts, dormer faces and small gables.
What the weather does
Low courses by the porch floor stay wet and rot at the corners.
How we match it
Three facets cut to the run that is left, laid on new wrap.

We open the wall before we price the rest.

Before you call

Ask us the hard one first.

Most calls start with one of these five.

Will you deal with my insurance company?

You talk to your insurer. We write the scope and send dated photos of each wall, so the adjuster sees what we see.

What if the wood under my old siding is rotten?

We open a test section first. Rot is shown to you, photographed and priced before we go further. It is never covered over.

My house was painted before 1978. Does that matter?

It can have lead paint. We tell you how that changes the prep and the cleanup before any scraping starts.

Cedar or vinyl: which is less work to keep?

Cedar needs stain or paint every several years and can be patched one shingle at a time. Vinyl needs a wash, not paint, and we keep real trim around the windows so it does not look flat.

How long does a whole house take?

A full cedar cape took about three weeks in one sample review. Repairs are shorter. The schedule is written into your price.
