## #patterns
- kicker: The pattern book · Plates 01–05
- h2: Five patterns on {{biz.city}} walls. Which one is on yours?
- lead: Pick the shingle you see in your gable or along your porch. Each plate says when it was used, how salt air wears it and how we match it.
- tabs: Straight · Staggered · Fishscale · Diamond · Hexagon
- plate labels: Era · Where you see it · What the weather does · How we match it · button "Match this pattern"
- 01 Straight (1900s–1920s capes and shingle-style houses): whole walls, the plainest run / bottom courses cup and split first, where splash and snow sit / we weave new shingles into the old joints so no seam lines up.
- 02 Staggered (1900s–1930s shingle style near the bay): butts set high and low on purpose / long butts catch a nor'easter and work loose first / we keep the high-low rhythm instead of laying a straight line through it.
- 03 Fishscale (1880s Queen Anne gables): front gables, towers, the band between floors / rounded butts wear thin and the curves break off one by one / we trace a scale still on the house and cut new ones to its curve and width.
- 04 Diamond (1880s–1890s Queen Anne and Stick accents): gable peaks, often mixed with fishscale / points split first and leave a ragged line you see from the street / new points cut to the old angle, primed on all sides before they go up.
- 05 Hexagon (1880s–1900s gables and porch skirts): porch skirts, dormer faces, small gables / low courses by the porch floor stay wet and rot at the corners / three facets cut to the run that is left, laid on new wrap.

## #faq
- kicker: Before you call
- h2: Ask us the hard one first.
- side line: Most calls start with one of these five.
- Q1 Will you deal with my insurance company? A: You talk to your insurer. We write the scope and send dated photos of each wall, so the adjuster sees what we see.
- Q2 What if the wood under my old siding is rotten? A: We open a test section first. Rot is shown to you, photographed and priced before we go further. It is never covered over.
- Q3 My house was painted before 1978. Does that matter? A: It can have lead paint. We tell you how that changes the prep and the cleanup before any scraping starts.
- Q4 Cedar or vinyl: which is less work to keep? A: Cedar needs stain or paint every several years and can be patched one shingle at a time. Vinyl needs a wash, not paint, and we keep real trim around the windows so it does not look flat.
- Q5 How long does a whole house take? A: A full cedar cape took about three weeks in one sample review. Repairs are shorter. The schedule is written into your price.
