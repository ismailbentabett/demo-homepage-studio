# Well Service — niche brief

## 1. The buyer
Who calls: homeowners on a private well, 35-70, living past the edge of city water: ranch homes on 1-20 acres,
farmhouses, new builds on rural lots, retirees who moved "out to the country". Three households in particular:
- the long-time owner whose pump is 15-25 years old and just quit (no water, dishes in the sink, kids need showers);
- the new buyer of a rural house who has never thought about where water comes from, and whose lender wants a well report;
- the person building on raw land who needs a new well before the slab goes in.

What just happened: taps went dry or sputter air; pressure surges and fades in the shower; the pump clicks on and off
every few seconds; the breaker keeps tripping; the water turned orange, smells like eggs, or tastes salty; a flood
covered the wellhead; the power came back after a storm and the pump didn't.

Fears, in their words:
- "They're going to tell me I need a whole new well and it's going to cost a fortune."
- "Are they just going to pull the pump and replace it without knowing what's wrong?"
- "Is my water even safe for the baby?"
- "What if they drill and hit nothing?" / "Is the water table dropping under us?"
- "I'm going to be without water all weekend."

What makes them trust: a person who answers and asks them to read the pressure gauge before sending a truck; readings and
numbers (depth, gallons a minute, PSI, amps) written down and handed over; prices as ranges before anyone shows up;
someone who explains the ground under their own county; reviews from towns they know by name.

What makes them leave in 5 seconds: a generic "water solutions" page that looks like a softener salesman; no phone number
visible; a drilling-rig stock photo from a mining site; no hint whether the company comes out to their town.

## 2. The job
Split: about 55% urgent (no water, low pressure, pump or control failure), 30% planned (pump swap before it dies, tank,
treatment, inspections for a home sale), 15% new wells and deepening.

Seasonality:
- Lubbock / South Plains: peak June-September (irrigation, gardens and stock tanks push pumps; the water table sits lowest
  in late summer, pumps pull air); spring wind and dust storms trip power; winter freezes crack exposed lines in well houses.
- Waco / Central Texas: summer peak July-September (100°F stretches, heavy use); spring thunderstorms (lightning, power
  surges) March-June; February freezes cause burst lines and frozen pressure switches.
- Tallahassee / Big Bend Florida: hurricane and tropical storm season June-November (outages, lightning, floodwater over
  wellheads); summer afternoon lightning almost daily; spring drought April-May; year-round sulfur and iron complaints.

Ticket bands: small (tank charge, switch, capacitor, water test), middle (pressure tank, pump replacement, treatment
system), large (constant-pressure system, well deepening), largest (new well).

Services that sell: new well drilling, well deepening, pump repair, pump replacement, pressure tank service, pressure
switch and controls, constant pressure systems, water testing, water treatment (softener, iron, sulfur, UV), well
inspections for home sales, shock chlorination, well abandonment, no-water calls.

Homeowner vs pro vocabulary:
- "no water" vs "pump failure / loss of prime / tripped overload"
- "the pump keeps clicking on and off" vs "short cycling, waterlogged pressure tank"
- "pressure goes weak then strong" vs "40/60 switch cycle, drawdown band"
- "water is sputtering air" vs "pumping below the static level / well drawdown"
- "the water level dropped" vs "declining static water level in the aquifer"
- "the box by the well" vs "control box / start capacitor"
- "rotten egg smell" vs "hydrogen sulfide"
- "orange stains" vs "iron and manganese, iron bacteria"
- "salty / hard water" vs "high TDS, sodium, calcium carbonate hardness"
- "how deep is my well" vs "total depth, pump set depth, static level"
- "how much water does it make" vs "yield in gallons per minute"
- "the pipe in the ground" vs "casing, well cap, sanitary seal"
- "closing an old well" vs "plugging and abandonment"
- "bleaching the well" vs "shock chlorination"

## 5. Niche tools (pick from, each site uses 2+)
1. **No-water triage.** Inputs: what the taps do (nothing / sputter / weak), the gauge reading, breaker state, recent
   storm or outage. Output: the likely cause (power, switch, tank, pump, water level) and what to check safely before
   calling. Leads to: "Call with this reading" button that prefills the form with the answers.
2. **Pressure gauge reader.** A dial the homeowner sets to what their gauge shows (and how it moves as a tap runs).
   Output: normal 40/60 cycle, short cycling (tank), never reaching cut-out (pump or leak), zero (power or pump).
   Leads to: the matching sample price range and the call.
3. **Depth and drill log explainer.** Input: ZIP or town. Output: a drawn cross-section of the typical layers and
   depth range in that area (caliche and Ogallala sand in Lubbock, Trinity limestone near Waco, Floridan limestone in
   Tallahassee), labelled typical, not a promise. Leads to: new well or deepening quote.
4. **Water test decoder.** Input: what they notice (smell, stain, taste, scale, spots, baby in the house). Output: what to
   test for and which treatment usually follows, with the sample price ranges. Leads to: book a test.
5. **Pump age check.** Inputs: year of install (or "came with the house"), cycling, noises. Output: repair vs replace
   leaning with sample ranges. Leads to: diagnostic call.
6. **Home sale well report.** Inputs: closing date, lender type. Output: what the report covers and the timeline to
   book before closing. Leads to: inspection booking.
7. **Storm restart checklist.** After an outage: the safe order of breaker, switch, gauge; when to stop and call.
   Leads to: call or text.

Prices are always ranges from `facts.proof.prices`, labelled sample.

## 6. The three directions

### A — We show you every foot of the hole, from caliche to water.   (axis: PROCESS — how the work is done, shown step by step)
- Brand: Static Level Water Wells · Lubbock, TX · facts-A.json
- Concept seed: On the South Plains the water sits deep in the Ogallala and drops a little every summer, so the whole
  site is a borehole: you scroll down through the ground, and each layer (topsoil, caliche, sand and gravel, the water,
  the red bed) is one step of how a well is sited, drilled, cased, pumped and tested. The homeowner sees the work as a
  drill log, the paper every Static Level customer gets.
- Tone lead: dark-led (deep night-earth brown-black, caliche bone white for text, one water blue that only appears once
  the scroll reaches the water)
- Type lead: condensed (a tall industrial condensed sans for depth numbers and headlines, plain grotesk body)
- Hero family: 12 — sticky photo, scrolling text (a tall photo of a drill mast against a wide West Texas dusk stays
  pinned while three short lines scroll past: "Site it.", "Drill it.", "Prove it with a log.")
- Signature seed: the depth rule. A left-edge ruler counts feet from 0 at the hero to about 320 at the contact block, with
  drawn strata bands behind each section; the static water line is a single blue rule that the site crosses once.
- Motion idea: intro: the depth rule draws down from 0 to 20 ft as the hero settles. Scroll story: the strata
  band behind the process section slides past while the depth counter ticks, and the water line fills blue when crossed.
- Tools: depth and drill log explainer (by town), pump age check, ZIP checker, multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: dusk and early morning on flat open land, wide horizon, red-brown soil, long shadows; close-ups of drill
  pipe, casing, cuttings and wet sand in warm low light. Nothing green and lush.
