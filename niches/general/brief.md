# General contractor — niche brief

## 1. The buyer

**Who calls.** Homeowners 32-70 with savings or a home-equity line, planning the biggest project they will ever do on the house. Four main types: (1) families who outgrew the house and would rather add on than move (addition, second story, finished basement); (2) owners of older houses (Nashville bungalows and Tudors, Madison's near-east-side four-squares, Asheville Arts & Crafts bungalows) ready for a kitchen and bath that work; (3) people who bought a dated house and want a whole-home renovation before they move in; (4) owners of a steep or awkward lot who want an ADU, a porch rebuild or a garage apartment.

**What just happened.**
- A baby is coming, a parent is moving in, or a teenager needs a room, and the house is one bedroom short.
- They closed on a 1960s ranch or a 1920s bungalow and the kitchen is original.
- They had a bad experience: a friend's contractor vanished halfway through with the deposit.
- Water in the basement after a big storm made them finally deal with it.
- Interest rates make moving expensive, so the money goes into the house they have.

**What they fear (in their words).**
- "They'll take the deposit and disappear for three weeks."
- "It'll cost double what they said and take twice as long."
- "I won't know what's happening on my own house until it's too late to change it."
- "Permits will hold everything up, or they'll skip them and I'll pay for it when I sell."
- "We'll be living in dust with no kitchen for six months."

**What makes them trust.**
- A real schedule with weeks, not "about four months", and a habit of reporting progress every week.
- Costs shown openly: what share goes to framing, plumbing, finishes, allowances, the contractor's fee.
- Clear talk about permits, inspections and who pulls them.
- One named person who answers their texts.
- Photos of real-looking stages (framing, rough-in, drywall), not just glossy finished kitchens.

**What makes them leave in 5 seconds.** A stock photo of a hard-hat handshake; "Building dreams since…"; a gallery of mansion kitchens far beyond their budget; no sense of what anything costs; no mention of permits; "Request a consultation" as the only way in; a site that reads like it builds office parks.

## 2. The job

**Emergency vs planned.** 100% planned. Lead times are long: design and permits can take 6-16 weeks before construction starts. No emergency line.

**Seasonality by month.**
| Month | Nashville (fast growth, infill) | Madison (cold winters, basements) | Asheville (mountains, steep lots, rebuilding) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan-Feb | Interior remodels, design and permits for spring additions | Interior and basement work; foundations wait for thaw | Interiors, design work, waiting on permits; ice days stop sites |
| Mar-Apr | Additions and foundations start; heavy booking | Frost lifts: foundations and additions break ground late April | Site work begins, spring rains slow grading |
| May-Jun | Peak framing season, porches and decks | Peak season starts, additions framed | Peak season, porches, ADUs |
| Jul-Aug | Framing continues; heat slows afternoons; storms | Peak season; exterior work before fall | Afternoon storms; steady exterior work |
| Sep-Oct | Strong exterior months; enclose before winter | Rush to get additions closed in before snow | Best weather; leaf season traffic |
| Nov-Dec | Interiors, kitchens and baths before holidays | Interiors, basements, kitchens | Interiors and finish work |

**Average ticket bands.**
- Small (rare for a GC): a bathroom, an egress window, a porch repair (tens of thousands).
- Medium: a kitchen, a finished basement, a big bath (tens of thousands to around $120k).
- Large: additions, whole-home renovations, ADUs, second stories (hundreds of thousands).

**Services that sell (from facts).** Kitchen remodels, bathroom remodels, home additions, basement finishing, whole-home renovations, porch and deck rebuilds, ADUs and garage apartments, permits and inspections handled, design-build, structural repairs, egress windows.

**Homeowner words vs pro words.**
| Homeowner says | Pro says |
|---|---|
| "the plan" / "the drawings" | construction documents, permit set |
| "how long will it take" | schedule, critical path, lead times |
| "the walls go up" | framing |
| "pipes and wires in the walls" | rough-in (plumbing, electrical, mechanical) |
| "the inspector comes" | framing / rough / final inspection |
| "money set aside for tile and fixtures" | allowances |
| "extra charges" | change orders |
| "your markup" | contractor's fee, overhead and profit |
| "the people you hire" | subcontractors, trades |
| "adding a room on" | addition, bump-out |
| "a little house in the backyard" | ADU, accessory dwelling unit, detached accessory structure |
| "the window in the basement you can climb out of" | egress window, window well |

## 4. What every competitor site does (so we do not)

1. Hard-hat handshake or a smiling man in a hard hat with blueprints.
2. "Building your dreams", "From concept to completion", "Quality craftsmanship".
3. A gallery of finished luxury kitchens with no prices or context.
4. A four-step icons row: Consult, Design, Build, Enjoy.
5. Logos of every trade association as the main proof.
6. "Request a consultation" as the only call to action.
7. No word on schedule, permits or how changes are priced.
8. Commercial projects mixed with homes so the visitor cannot tell who it is for.

## 5. Niche tools (pick from, each site uses 2+)

1. **Project timeline builder.** Inputs: project type (kitchen, bath, basement, addition, whole home), size (S/M/L), start month. Output: a week-by-week schedule with typical phases (design, permits, demo, framing, rough-ins, inspections, drywall, finishes, final), labelled typical; ends with the expected move-back-in week.
2. **Budget split.** Inputs: total budget, project type. Output: a bar showing typical shares (demo, structure, mechanicals, finishes, allowances, contractor's fee, contingency), with the method shown and "typical, not a quote".
3. **Permit checker.** Inputs: what you are changing (walls, plumbing, electrical, windows, square footage, basement bedroom). Output: which work usually needs a permit and which inspections follow, in plain words, with "your city decides" and a link-out note.
4. **Cost range estimator.** Inputs: project type and size. Output: a sample range built from facts.proof.prices only, with per-sq-ft math shown where the price is per sq ft.
5. **Weekly report preview.** A sample weekly site report (done this week, next week, decisions needed, photos taken) so the visitor sees how they will be kept informed.
6. **Lot and slope check** (mountain sites). Inputs: steep yes/no, access, near a creek. Output: what usually affects cost (grading, retaining, foundations), plain words.
7. **Live-in-or-move-out guide.** Inputs: project type, kids, home office. Output: which weeks are hard to live through (no kitchen, no water, dust) for planning.
8. **ZIP checker** + **multi-step quote form** (every site).

## 6. The three directions

### A — Every week is a tie on the track, and you get a report for each one   (axis: PROCESS — how the work is done, shown step by step)
- Brand: Crosstie Building Co. · Nashville, TN · facts-A.json
- Concept seed: Nashville is a city of rail yards and fast infill. Crosstie's process is not "plan becomes build", it is a rhythm: every week of a job is one crosstie laid down, every Friday the client gets a site report (what got done, what is next, what they need to decide). The homepage is the track of a typical addition: week 1 demo, week 3 footings, week 6 framing inspection, week 12 drywall, week 18 walk-through. The promise is not a dream, it is that you never go a week without knowing.
- Tone lead: **dark-led** (deep creosote black-brown and rail-steel gray as the field, warm work-light amber for the "today" line and stamps, chalk white text).
- Type lead: **condensed** (a strong industrial condensed sans for the week numbers and headlines, like stenciling on a rail car; a readable sans for reports).
- Hero family: **1 — full-bleed video + scrim** (a slow full-bleed clip of framing going up at dusk under work lights, a dark scrim, the headline set as the week count: "Week 1 to week 20. A report every Friday.").
- Signature seed: **the page is a track of crossties.** Every section is a tie: a horizontal bar with the week number stenciled on its end, the phase name, and a small "Friday report" card (done, next, decisions). Services are the kinds of track (kitchen line, addition line, basement line), reviews arrive as quoted lines from weekly reports, and prices sit at the stations where money is decided. Inspections appear as signal posts between ties.
- Motion idea: intro: the video fades up from black as two steel rails draw themselves down the left edge. Scroll story: a glowing amber "today" line moves along the track as you scroll, and each tie it crosses flips its report card from "upcoming" to "done" with a stamp.
- Tools: project timeline builder, weekly report preview, live-in-or-move-out guide, + ZIP checker + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: dusk and work lights, framing silhouettes, rough-in close-ups (wires, pipes, inspection tags without readable text), clean job-site order; Nashville bungalows and infill houses; serious and steady, no smiling hard hats.

### B — Every dollar on the table, line by line   (axis: PROOF & TOOLS — numbers, checks, answers before the call)
- Brand: Open Ledger Builders · Madison, WI · facts-B.json
- Concept seed: Madison homeowners read the fine print. Open Ledger runs every job open-book: the client sees the same cost sheet the builder sees, with each trade's line, the allowances, the contingency and the builder's fee as its own honest line. The site does the same thing in public: sample budgets split line by line, a permit checker, and the egress-window rule that decides whether a basement room can be a bedroom.
- Tone lead: **light-led** (paper white and ledger cream with fine ruled lines, ink in deep blue-black, one muted red for totals and the fee line).
- Type lead: **grotesk** (a clean neutral grotesk with tabular figures, like a well-made spreadsheet; headlines set large and quiet).
- Hero family: **2 — 50/50 split** (left half: the headline and a short open-book promise; right half: a sample budget ledger that fills in line by line, with a real-looking but clearly sample basement or kitchen budget).
- Signature seed: **the page is an open ledger.** Every section is a line item with a percent-of-budget column on the right edge: "Framing and structure — 18%", "Plumbing, electrical, HVAC — 22%", "Allowances you choose — 25%", "Our fee — 12%". The column adds up to 100% by the end of the page, and the footer is the total line. Services, reviews ("the final number was 3% over, and we saw why") and prices are posted as entries with a date and a check mark.
- Motion idea: intro: the ledger lines rule themselves across the right half, then figures type in one by one with a soft tick. Scroll story: a running total in the margin grows as each section's percentage is posted, and a thin bar chart at the top of the viewport fills segment by segment until it reads 100%.
- Tools: budget split, permit checker, cost range estimator, + ZIP checker + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: calm daylight interiors in Upper Midwest homes, basements being finished (framing, insulation, egress wells), kitchens mid-build and finished, paper plans and tape measures on a table; honest, organized, nothing flashy.

### C — Building on the mountain, one contour at a time   (axis: LOCAL STORY — the place, the weather, the streets, the people)
- Brand: Switchback Building Co. · Asheville, NC · facts-C.json
- Concept seed: In Asheville, almost no lot is flat. Old Arts & Crafts bungalows sit on hillsides in Montford and West Asheville, new ADUs climb backyards, porches need rebuilding on steep grades, and after Helene many neighbors are repairing what the water damaged. Switchback is a builder who knows the slopes: foundations, retaining, drainage, access for a crew on a narrow road. Tell the story respectfully: practical repair and rebuilding help, no fear marketing, no storm-chasing tone.
- Tone lead: **color-led** (a deep Blue Ridge slate blue as the field, with moss and rhododendron-leaf tones, and a warm rust for contour labels; text on cream panels).
- Type lead: **slab** (a sturdy slab serif with character for headlines, like a trail sign carved in wood; a humanist sans for body).
- Hero family: **15 — map / area-led** (the hero is a stylized topo map of Asheville and the surrounding towns, contour lines in fine rust, with the headline set along a ridge and the ZIP checker built into the map: type a ZIP and the town lights up).
- Signature seed: **the page climbs contour lines.** Each section sits on its own contour band with an elevation label (2,100 ft, 2,200 ft…), and the content climbs as you scroll: the base is the river (repairs after flooding), then the hillside bungalows (porches, kitchens), then steep lots (ADUs, additions, retaining), then the ridge (the whole team, the area served). Services, reviews and prices are placed as trail markers on the contours; the service area is the map itself.
- Motion idea: intro: contour lines draw outward from the city center, then town names fade in at their spots. Scroll story: the elevation counter in the corner climbs as you scroll and the contours slowly shift, like walking up a switchback trail; each section's trail marker drops in when it reaches the line.
- Tools: lot and slope check, project timeline builder, cost range estimator, + ZIP checker (in the map) + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: mountain light and mist, Arts & Crafts porches with stone piers, framing on a steep lot, ridgelines in the distance, rebuilt porches and new ADUs; respectful, no flood-damage spectacle, no people's faces.
