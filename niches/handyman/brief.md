# Handyman — niche brief

## 1. The buyer

**Who calls.** Homeowners 30-75 with a list, not a project. Four main types: (1) busy two-income households with a running list on the fridge (a door that sticks, a loose railing, a TV to mount, a leaky faucet); (2) older owners who used to do it themselves and no longer climb ladders; (3) people selling or buying, with an inspection report full of small items to clear; (4) landlords and owners of older houses (Grand Rapids four-squares, Ann Arbor rentals near campus, Louisville shotgun and camelback houses) where something small breaks every month.

**What just happened.**
- The home inspection came back with 23 small items and closing is in three weeks.
- Guests are coming (Thanksgiving, a graduation, Derby weekend) and the list suddenly matters.
- A storm door blew off its closer, a gutter came loose, a stair rail wobbles.
- They bought a new TV, a flat-pack wardrobe or a ceiling fan and have no time or tools.
- The plumber and electrician they called said the job is "too small".

**What they fear (in their words).**
- "Nobody calls back for a small job."
- "They'll say a window between 8 and 5 and show up at 4."
- "I'll pay a trip charge and they'll do one thing and leave."
- "They'll do a sloppy patch and I'll have to redo it."
- "They'll try something they shouldn't, like rewiring a panel."

**What makes them trust.**
- A clear visit: arrival window, how long, what fits in that time.
- Prices for common jobs or by the hour, with what counts as one visit.
- Honesty about what they do not do (panel work, gas lines, major plumbing go to licensed trades).
- Photos of real small jobs: a clean drywall patch, a straight shelf, a re-hung door.
- One person who answers texts, and a truck that comes with the right parts.

**What makes them leave in 5 seconds.** A cartoon handyman with a toolbelt; "No job too small!" in a starburst; a phone number and nothing else; no prices at all; "we do everything" including roofs and electrical panels; stock photos of a man smiling with a drill; a contact form with no word about when they will come.

## 2. The job

**Emergency vs planned.** Mostly planned and booked days to two weeks ahead. Some urgent-but-small calls (a door that won't lock, a loose stair rail, a leak under a sink) get the next open slot, but there is no emergency line on these sites.

**Seasonality by month.**
| Month | Grand Rapids (snow belt, older homes) | Ann Arbor (college town, rentals, busy owners) | Louisville (river city, old shotgun houses, Derby) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan-Feb | Indoor lists: drywall, doors, shelves, drafty windows | Indoor lists; landlords between semesters | Indoor work; drafty old windows and doors |
| Mar-Apr | Storm doors, screens, gutters after snow load | Move-out repairs at rentals start | Derby-ready rush: porches, paint touch-ups, screen doors (Derby is the first Saturday in May) |
| May-Jun | Decks, fences, screens, outdoor lists | Move-out and move-in repairs, graduations | Porches, decks, fans before the heat |
| Jul-Aug | Decks, exterior touch-ups | Turnover between leases (heavy) | Heat slows exterior work; ceiling fans, indoor jobs |
| Sep-Oct | Weatherstripping, gutters, storm doors before snow | Fall lists, football weekends with guests | Fall exterior lists, gutters |
| Nov-Dec | Holiday guest lists, TV mounting, lights | Holiday lists, quiet campus | Holiday lists, indoor work |

**Average ticket bands.**
- Small: one item, an hour or two (a faucet, a TV mount, a door adjustment), around $100-$300.
- Medium: a half-day list of 4-8 items, around $300-$500.
- Large: a full day or two (inspection lists, a rental turnover, a porch board and rail repair), around $600-$1,800.

**Services that sell (from facts).** Drywall patches and paint touch-ups, door and window fixes, TV and shelf mounting, furniture assembly, minor plumbing (faucets, toilets, caulk), light fixture and fan swaps, deck and fence repair, porch board and rail repair, weatherstripping, inspection repair lists, rental turnover lists.

**Homeowner words vs pro words.**
| Homeowner says | Pro says |
|---|---|
| "my list" / "the honey-do list" | punch list, work order |
| "the hole in the wall" | drywall patch, California patch, mesh patch |
| "the door won't close" | door out of square, hinge shim, strike plate adjust |
| "the toilet keeps running" | flapper, fill valve |
| "the caulk is black around the tub" | re-caulk, mildew-resistant silicone |
| "a light that doesn't work" | fixture swap (existing box), not new circuits |
| "how long will it take" | labor hours, half-day / full-day block |
| "a trip charge" | minimum visit, service call |
| "the stuff from the inspection" | inspection repair addendum, repair request |
| "the porch floor is soft" | rotted decking, joist sistering |
| "the cold air comes in under the door" | door sweep, weatherstripping, threshold adjustment |

**Honest limits to say on every site.** Electrical panel work, new circuits, gas lines, main water lines and structural changes go to licensed trades. Rules differ by state and city (Michigan, for example, requires a license for residential alteration jobs over a set dollar amount); the site says "we tell you when a job needs a licensed trade" instead of claiming a credential.

## 4. What every competitor site does (so we do not)

1. A cartoon or clip-art handyman, or a smiling man with a drill in a studio.
2. "No job too small!" and "Your one-stop shop for all home repairs".
3. A grid of 30 service icons including roofing, HVAC and electrical panels.
4. No prices, not even an hourly rate or a minimum.
5. "Call for a free estimate" as the only way in.
6. No word about arrival windows or how long a visit is.
7. A gallery of big remodels the handyman did not do.
8. Reviews that only say "great job, very nice".

## 5. Niche tools (pick from, each site uses 2+)

1. **Punch-list builder.** Inputs: pick items from a list (patch drywall, mount TV, re-hang door, swap faucet, assemble furniture, caulk tub…), with a size or count. Output: a typical hour band per item and a total hour band, then which visit fits (2 hours, half day, full day), labelled "typical, not a quote".
2. **Half-day planner.** Inputs: the list. Output: whether it fits a 4-hour visit, the order the work would run in, and what to have ready (parts, paint color, clear space).
3. **Fix-or-specialist triage.** Inputs: what is wrong (no power in a room, leak, soft floor, door). Output: "a handyman can do this", "this needs a licensed electrician/plumber", or "have it looked at first", in plain words.
4. **Photo-to-quote checklist.** Guides the visitor on which photos to take for a quote (wide shot, close-up, a tape measure in frame, the model number) so a price can be given by text. No upload needed; it builds the list.
5. **Price by visit / by job.** A table built from facts.proof.prices only, labelled "sample".
6. **Seasonal or event checklist.** A list of small fixes before winter, before guests, or before Derby; tick items and get an hour band.
7. **Inspection list sorter.** Paste or tick inspection items; sorts them into handyman / licensed trade / ask your agent.
8. **ZIP checker** + **multi-step quote form** (every site).

## 6. The three directions

### A — Punch in at 8:00, punch out at 12:00, list done   (axis: PROCESS — how the work is done, shown step by step)
- Brand: Punchclock Repair Co. · Grand Rapids, MI · facts-A.json
- Concept seed: Not a plan or a build story: a single morning. Punchclock sells the half-day visit. The tech punches in at 8:00 sharp, walks the list with you for ten minutes, works it in an agreed order, shows you each finished item, sweeps up and punches out by 12:00. The homepage is that morning, minute by minute: 8:00 arrive, 8:10 walk the list, 8:25 first fix, 10:30 the door that needed shims, 11:40 the walk-back, 11:55 clean up, 12:00 punch out with the list ticked. The promise is time you can plan around.
- Tone lead: **light-led** (time-card buff and paper white as the field, ink black text, a punch-clock red for time stamps and the "now" mark, a little steel gray).
- Type lead: **mono-accent** (a typewriter-style monospace for every time stamp, card label and price, over a clean humanist sans for body and a firm sans for headlines).
- Hero family: **10 — centered statement + photo marquee** (a centered headline, "8:00 to 12:00. Your list, done.", with a stamped time card under it, and a slow marquee of small-job photos running below: a patched wall, a mounted shelf, a re-hung door, a new faucet).
- Signature seed: **the page is a time card for one visit.** Every section has a stamped time in the left margin (8:00, 8:10, 8:25…), like punches on a card, and a thin vertical time rail connects them. Services are what gets done in each slot, reviews are notes written on the card ("done by 11:40"), prices are the card's totals (half day, full day, per hour). The footer is the punch-out stamp at 12:00 with the call button.
- Motion idea: intro: the time card slides up and "8:00" is stamped onto it with a small press effect. Scroll story: a clock hand in the corner advances from 8:00 to 12:00 as you scroll, and each section's time stamp inks in as it is reached.
- Tools: half-day planner, punch-list builder, price by visit, + ZIP checker + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: bright morning light in real Midwest homes, small finished fixes close up (a patch you cannot see, a level shelf, a door that closes), a tidy tool bag on a drop cloth, hands at work; calm and organized, no posing.

### B — Every tool on the wall, every job with an hour count   (axis: PROOF & TOOLS — numbers, checks, answers before the call)
- Brand: Pegboard Home Repair · Ann Arbor, MI · facts-B.json
- Concept seed: Ann Arbor households are busy and like to know before they ask. Pegboard puts everything on the wall: each common job is a tool outline on a pegboard with its typical hours and a sample price hanging under it. Visitors build their own punch list, see the hour band, check which items need a licensed trade, and get a list of photos to text for a firm price. Proof is the tools, not the talk.
- Tone lead: **color-led** (a strong pegboard tan or a deep workshop teal as the field, with tool outlines in a sharp safety yellow and white labels; dark text on light tags).
- Type lead: **condensed** (a tall condensed sans for headlines and tag labels, like stencils on a shop wall; a plain sans for the body).
- Hero family: **8 — photo mosaic** (a grid of small-job photos laid out like tools on a pegboard, each tile a different job, with the headline on a tag in the middle: "Pick your jobs. See the hours.").
- Signature seed: **the page is a pegboard.** The background is a fine grid of peg holes; every section hangs on pegs with a painted tool outline (hammer for repairs, drill for mounting, wrench for plumbing, tape for measuring). Services are tools with hang tags showing hour bands, reviews are notes pinned to the board, prices are tags under each tool. When a visitor adds a job to their list, its tool "comes off the wall" and the outline stays, the way a real shop shows what is in use.
- Motion idea: intro: the mosaic tiles drop onto their pegs one by one with a small swing. Scroll story: hang tags swing gently into place as each section enters; adding an item to the punch list lifts its tool off the board into a list tray at the bottom of the screen.
- Tools: punch-list builder (hour bands), fix-or-specialist triage, photo-to-quote checklist, + ZIP checker + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: clean workshop and home details in daylight, tools in hands, finished small jobs (TV mounted level, flat-pack wardrobe built, faucet installed), Ann Arbor-style older homes and rentals; bright, practical, no faces.

### C — The neighborhood walk, from Old Louisville to the Highlands   (axis: LOCAL STORY — the place, the weather, the streets, the people)
- Brand: Camelback Repair Co. · Louisville, KY · facts-C.json
- Concept seed: Louisville is a city of narrow shotgun houses and camelbacks (a shotgun with a second story at the back), brick Victorians in Old Louisville, cottages in Germantown and bungalows in the Highlands. Camelback knows these houses: porches that rot at the front edge, screen doors that sag, plaster walls that crack, tall ceilings that need a long ladder for a fan. And every spring the city gets ready for Derby: the front porch has to look right for the first Saturday in May. The homepage walks the neighborhoods, street by street, with the small fixes each house type needs, and ends with a Derby-ready list.
- Tone lead: **dark-led** (deep bourbon-barrel brown and brick red-black as the field, warm brass and porch-light amber accents, cream text; a lighter cream panel for lists).
- Type lead: **slab** (a sturdy slab serif with old-sign character for headlines; a warm sans for body).
- Hero family: **5 — offset framed photo** (one photo of a shotgun or camelback house porch, framed off-center like a picture hung slightly low on a plaster wall, with the headline beside it: "Small fixes for old Louisville houses.").
- Signature seed: **the page is a walk through the neighborhoods.** Each section is a stop on the walk with a street-sign style marker (Old Louisville, Germantown, the Highlands, Crescent Hill) and the house type found there; services are the fixes those houses need, reviews come from neighbors on that street, prices are posted on a porch-post tag. The walk ends at a front porch with the Derby-ready checklist: tick the items, see the hour band, book before the first Saturday in May.
- Motion idea: intro: the framed photo swings slightly and settles, like a picture hung on a nail. Scroll story: a dotted walking path runs down the page between stops and draws itself as you scroll; each street marker flips up when the path reaches it.
- Tools: Derby-ready checklist (seasonal checklist with hour band), fix-or-specialist triage for old houses, price by job, + ZIP checker + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: warm late-afternoon light on porches, brick and painted wood, tall plaster rooms with ceiling fans, screen doors, porch floorboards, old door hardware; respectful of old houses, no faces, no house numbers or readable street signs in photos (the street markers are drawn, not photographed).
