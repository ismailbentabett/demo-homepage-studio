# Concrete — niche brief

Three sites, three very different ground conditions: Salt Lake City (freeze-thaw, road salt, cold-weather pours),
Mesa (desert heat, caliche, RV pads and pool decks), Colorado Springs (swelling clay, 6,000+ ft, deicer damage).
The page must feel like it was poured by someone who knows the dirt under *this* town.

## 1. The buyer

**Who calls.** Homeowners 35-70, mostly in owner-occupied single-family homes 15-60 years old. Three common situations:
- The driveway or patio has failed: a slab heaved, sank toward the house, cracked into plates, or the top is flaking off after winter.
- A planned project: a bigger patio for a new grill/pergola, an RV or boat pad (very common in Mesa and the Treasure Valley-style
  suburbs of Utah), a wider driveway for a third car, a walkway to a side gate.
- Selling or just bought: a home inspector flagged a trip hazard, negative grading or a cracked garage slab.
Couples decide together; one partner researches on a phone at night, the other wants a number.

**What they are afraid of (their words).**
1. "It'll crack in a year and they'll say concrete always cracks."
2. "They'll pour it thin, skip the gravel and I'll never know until it breaks."
3. "They'll tear up my lawn and sprinklers and leave the mess."
4. "The quote will be one number with no breakdown, then it'll go up on pour day."
5. "Water will still run toward my foundation."

**What makes them trust.**
1. Thickness, base depth, steel and mix written on the quote (4 in. vs 5-6 in., wire vs rebar vs fiber, air-entrained or not).
2. Photos of the *forms and base* before the pour, not only shiny finished slabs.
3. Someone explains control joints: where the slab will crack on purpose and why.
4. A clear plan for slope/drainage and for the sprinkler lines and lawn edge.
5. A local who talks about local trouble: road salt, swelling clay, summer pours at dawn.

**What makes them leave in 5 seconds.** Stock photo of a commercial parking garage or a highway pour; a cement-mixer truck as the
hero; "Quality concrete you can trust"; no city visible; no way to get even a rough number; a wall of text about "decorative solutions".

## 2. The job

**Emergency vs planned.** ~95% planned. Nobody needs a 2 a.m. concrete call; the urgent part is "before winter" or "before we list the house".
`emergency: false` on all three sites.

**Seasonality.**
| Month | Salt Lake City | Mesa | Colorado Springs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan-Feb | Few pours (blankets, heated water), lots of bids for spring | Prime pour season, cool mornings | Few pours, inspection of winter damage |
| Mar-Apr | Phones start; damage from salt shows | Busy; last comfortable months | Spring bids; slab lifting after thaw |
| May-Jun | Peak | Pours move to 4-5 a.m. starts as heat climbs | Peak |
| Jul-Aug | Peak, hot afternoons | Hottest: dawn pours, retarders, wet cure; monsoon storms can stop a pour | Peak; afternoon hail/thunderstorm risk |
| Sep-Oct | "Before the snow" rush | Busy again as it cools | "Before the freeze" rush |
| Nov-Dec | Cold-weather pours only, sealing season ends | Good pour season | Mostly done; garage floors, interior work |

**Ticket bands (words).** Small repairs (crack sealing, a step, slab lifting) are low hundreds to low thousands. A walkway or small patio
is low thousands. A full driveway tear-out and repour, or an RV pad, is mid-thousands to low tens of thousands. Stamped or colored
patios and big pool decks run highest per square foot.

**Services that sell (from facts).** Driveway tear-out and repour, new patios, walkways and side paths, stamped and colored concrete,
RV and boat pads, slab lifting, crack repair and resurfacing, garage floors, steps and porches, pool decks (Mesa), sealing.

**Homeowner words vs pro words.**
| Homeowner says | Pro says |
|---|---|
| "The driveway is cracking into pieces" | Random cracking, missing or late-cut control joints |
| "The top is flaking off" | Surface scaling (freeze-thaw + deicer, poor air entrainment or over-finishing) |
| "Little holes popped out" | Popouts (reactive or porous aggregate near the surface) |
| "One slab sank / tilts toward the house" | Settlement, loss of subgrade support, negative slope |
| "The sidewalk lifted" | Heave (frost heave or expansive soil swell, tree roots) |
| "How thick is it?" | Slab thickness, 4 in. residential / 5-6 in. for vehicles |
| "The gravel under it" | Compacted aggregate base course, road base |
| "The metal inside" | Reinforcement: rebar on chairs, welded wire, or fiber |
| "The lines cut in it" | Control (contraction) joints, saw-cut within 6-18 hours |
| "The rough/swept look" | Broom finish |
| "Stones showing on top" | Exposed aggregate |
| "Fake stone pattern" | Stamped concrete with release agent and integral/cast-on color |
| "Raising the sunken slab" | Slab jacking: mudjacking or polyurethane foam lifting |
| "The cool stuff by the pool" | Cool deck / knockdown texture (Mesa) |
| "Waiting before we drive on it" | Cure time: walk on in ~1-2 days, cars ~7 days, full design strength ~28 days |

## 4. What every competitor site does (so we do not)

1. A ready-mix truck chute or a pump boom as the hero, often with a visible brand on the drum.
2. "Quality concrete you can trust" / "No job too big or small" / "Built to last".
3. A gallery of 40 identical finished slabs, no captions, half commercial.
4. A slider of "decorative concrete" photos with Pinterest-y wet-look sealer shine.
5. A "Free estimate" button as the only way to learn anything; no ranges, no thickness, no base talk.
6. Mention of "residential and commercial flatwork" first, as if the homeowner were an afterthought.
7. Stock handshake or a smiling crew posed with arms crossed by a truck.

## 5. Niche tools (pick from, each site uses 2+)

1. **Slab calculator.** Inputs: length, width, thickness (4/5/6 in.), use (walk, patio, car, RV). Output: square feet, cubic yards
   (+10% waste), suggested thickness for the use, and a *sample* price range from `facts.proof.prices` for the matching item.
   Leads to the call: "Send me these numbers" pre-fills the quote form.
2. **Crack reader.** Pick the picture that looks like yours (hairline web, straight crack at a joint, one slab lower than the other,
   flaky surface, little craters, lifted edge). Output: what it usually means, whether it is cosmetic or structural, repair vs replace,
   and which service matches. CTA "Have us look at it".
3. **Finish compare.** Slider/tabs: broom, exposed aggregate, stamped, colored, cool deck. Output: look, grip when wet, upkeep
   (resealing every 2-3 years for stamped), sample price per sq ft.
4. **Lift or replace quiz.** 5 questions (how much it sank, cracked into pieces?, age, drainage toward house?, trees nearby). Output:
   slab lifting candidate vs tear-out, with the reason.
5. **Pour season planner.** Pick a month: what a pour looks like then in this city (dawn start and retarder in Mesa July; blankets
   and heated water in Salt Lake January; hail watch in Colorado Springs July). Static, honest facts; no weather feed.
6. **Winter damage reader** (cold cities). Scaling vs popouts vs heave vs settling, with the cause and the fix, plus a "salt safety" note
   (no deicers on new concrete the first winter; sand for traction).
7. **RV / boat pad sizer** (Mesa). Pick vehicle length + side clearance + gate width. Output: pad size, thickness, approach notes,
   sample range.
8. **Cure clock.** Enter pour date: shows walk-on day, car day, ~28-day strength day, first-seal window. Pure date math.

All tools: ranges only from `facts.proof.prices`, labelled **Sample pricing**; every output ends with "Your real number comes from a
visit: we measure, check the base and the slope."

## 6. The three directions

### A — "A slab is decided in the dirt, not the truck."   (axis: PROCESS)
- Brand: Long Cure Concrete Co. · Salt Lake City, UT · facts-A.json
- Concept seed: The company's whole pitch is patience: the part under the slab and the days after the pour. The homepage shows
  the job as the carbon copy of a real job ticket: every step a line item you can read, from the dig to the 28th day of cure.
  It makes the invisible half of a slab (base, steel, joints, curing) the hero instead of the shiny finish.
- Tone lead: **light-led** (ticket paper off-white, carbon blue ink, one safety-orange accent for stamps).
- Type lead: **mono-accent** (a crisp grotesk for body/headings with a typewriter-ish mono for ticket lines, quantities, codes).
- Hero family: **14 — document object as hero.** The hero *is* a long pour ticket on the page: job lines (EXCAVATE 6 IN. · BASE 4 IN.
  ROAD BASE, COMPACTED · STEEL #4 REBAR 18 IN. O.C. ON CHAIRS · MIX 4,000 PSI, AIR-ENTRAINED · JOINTS SAW-CUT SAME DAY · CURE 7 DAYS
  WET + COMPOUND) with a photo of a broom finish clipped to it and a stamp "SAMPLE TICKET". Headline sits above the ticket.
- Signature seed: **The page is a carbon-copy pour ticket.** Every section is a numbered line item (01 DIG … 08 CURE) with a
  perforated left edge, a carbon-blue duplicate offset on hover, and a 28-day strength curve as the scroll progress bar in the right
  margin (day 0 → day 28, "walk on", "drive on", "full strength" ticks). Prices, tools and the form are lines on the same ticket;
  the footer is the customer copy with the signature line.
- Motion idea: Intro: the ticket feeds up out of a slot (clip-path reveal) line by line and the "SCHEDULED" rubber stamp lands
  (scale 1.08 → 1, 200 ms, no bounce). Scroll story: a pinned slab cross-section builds layer by layer (subgrade, compacted base,
  chairs + rebar, concrete, broom texture, saw-cut joint, curing blanket) as the line items tick from 01 to 08.
- Tools: Slab calculator, Crack reader, Cure clock + ZIP checker + multi-step quote form (the form is "ticket page 2").
- Photo mood: overcast or early-morning flat light, close distance, raking light across fresh broom texture, forms and string lines,
  cold-season details (curing blankets, frost on a lawn edge). Grey-blue, no golden-hour gloss.

### B — "Every promise is a number you can check."   (axis: PROOF & TOOLS)
- Brand: Fieldtest Concrete Co. · Mesa, AZ · facts-B.json
- Concept seed: In Mesa everyone's slab "looks fine" for a year; the difference is thickness, steel, base and how it was cured in
  110° heat. This company shows the numbers and lets the homeowner build their own spec before calling: size, thickness, finish,
  pad for the RV. Every claim on the page is a measurement with a dimension line, and the homeowner's choices collect into a
  printable spec that pre-fills the quote.
- Tone lead: **dark-led** (charcoal basalt base, hot dawn amber accent, bone-white type).
- Type lead: **condensed** (tall condensed display for giant numerals and dimensions, plain sans body).
- Hero family: **3 — giant type mask over photo.** Huge condensed "4 IN." (or "6 IN." for the RV pad line) masks a dawn photo of
  fresh concrete being screeded; on scroll the mask opens to the full photo. Subline: what, where, and the "Build your spec" button.
- Signature seed: **The spec rail.** A sticky right rail (bottom sheet on phones) titled "Your slab" fills in as the visitor uses the
  page: size from the calculator, thickness from the use, finish from the compare, pad options. Every section ends with a measured
  callout (dimension lines with arrowheads and a value), and each callout can be "added to my spec". The quote form is the spec rail
  opened full-screen.
- Motion idea: Intro: the giant numerals rise and the photo slides inside them, then the mask scales open (clip-path, 900 ms).
  Scroll story: a core-sample cylinder descends through a pinned panel; each layer labels itself with a dimension (finish, slab,
  steel position, base, subgrade) and the matching spec line lights up in the rail.
- Tools: Slab calculator (feeds the rail), Finish compare (broom / exposed aggregate / stamped / cool deck), RV & boat pad sizer,
  Pour season planner (dawn pours, retarders, wet cure in summer) + ZIP checker + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: desert dawn, long low shadows, amber sky edge, dark foreground, macro of aggregate and steel, wet curing sheen.
  Hard light, high contrast.

### C — "Concrete for swelling clay and a hundred frozen nights."   (axis: LOCAL STORY)
- Brand: Red Grit Concrete · Colorado Springs, CO · facts-C.json
- Concept seed: Colorado Springs sits at 6,000+ feet on expansive clay, under deicer spray and hard freeze-thaw swings, in the
  shadow of Pikes Peak's pink granite. The site tells the story of concrete here, town by town and elevation by elevation, and shows
  why a Springs slab gets air-entrained mix, a thicker base, real joints and a sealer schedule. The service map is the hero.
- Tone lead: **color-led** (Pikes Peak granite red/terracotta as the field color, pine green, snow white type).
- Type lead: **slab** (a sturdy slab serif for headlines like trail signs; humanist sans body).
- Hero family: **15 — map / area-led hero.** A stylised topographic map of the Pikes Peak region: contour lines, the service towns
  pinned with their approximate elevations (Fountain ~5,550 ft, Colorado Springs ~6,035 ft, Manitou Springs ~6,400 ft, Monument
  ~6,975 ft, Woodland Park ~8,465 ft), the ZIP checker built into the map, and a headline on the left.
- Signature seed: **The contour system.** The whole page is a topo sheet: soft contour lines run behind every section, each section
  header is an elevation marker like a trail sign ("6,035 FT · YOUR DRIVEWAY IN JANUARY"), the page "climbs" as you scroll (the elevation
  readout in the corner counts up), and every photo caption carries a small benchmark triangle. Reviews are pinned on the map by town.
- Motion idea: Intro: contour lines draw in (stroke-dashoffset, staggered), then town pins drop and the headline fades up. Scroll
  story: "One January night on a driveway": pinned photo of a frosted slab with a 4-step diagram (melt water enters pores → freezes and
  expands ~9% → surface scales → what air-entrained mix + sealer do) advancing with scroll.
- Tools: Winter damage reader, Lift or replace quiz, ZIP checker on the map + multi-step quote form.
- Photo mood: crisp high-altitude light, deep blue sky, red rock and granite grit, snow-dusted concrete edges, late afternoon warmth,
  ponderosa pine. Real Front Range ranch and split-level homes.

**Local facts to use (C):** Colorado Springs elevation ~6,035 ft; expansive (swelling) clay and claystone are common across parts of
El Paso County; winters bring many freeze-thaw swings and magnesium-chloride road deicer; summer afternoon thunderstorms and hail.
Do not invent soil maps or say a given neighborhood "has bentonite". Say "common in the area", "we test your yard".

**Local facts (A, Salt Lake City):** Valley winters with inversions, frequent freeze-thaw, road salt; snowmelt from canyons; many
1950s-70s ranch and rambler homes with original driveways; spring is when the scaling shows. Cold-weather pours need blankets and
protected curing; no deicers on new concrete the first winter.

**Local facts (B, Mesa):** Summer highs over 105-110°F; pours start before sunrise in summer; caliche (hard calcium carbonate soil layer)
can slow digging; RV gates and RV pads are common on larger Mesa/Gilbert lots; pool decks with cool-deck texture; monsoon season
roughly mid-June to September.
