# Consulting — niche brief

Small business consulting for owner-run businesses: a bakery with two locations, a landscaping company with
eleven trucks, a machine shop with a founder who is 63. The "homeowner" of this niche is the owner of a small
business. They do not buy "strategy"; they buy a calmer Monday, a payroll that clears, a price they stop
apologising for, and a plan for the day they step back.

## 1. The buyer

**Who calls.** Owners of businesses with 3 to 40 people, roughly 35 to 65 years old. Most started as the person
who did the work (the baker, the electrician, the designer) and became the person who does the books at 10 p.m.
Many are second-generation owners or couples who run the business together. A smaller group are owners in their
late 50s and 60s who are starting to ask what happens to the business when they stop.

**What just happened that made them search.**
- Payroll was tight two months in a row, even though the shop was busy.
- A key employee quit and the owner realised nobody else knows how half the work gets done.
- The accountant said "your margins are thin" at tax time and offered nothing past that sentence.
- They raised prices for the first time in four years and are scared of the phone calls.
- A bank, a landlord or a partner asked for "a plan" and they have never written one.
- A child or a long-time manager asked, "Are you going to sell, or hand it to us?"

**What they are afraid of (their words).**
1. "A consultant will come in, use words I don't understand, hand me a binder, and leave."
2. "It'll cost more than the problem it fixes, and I won't know until the invoice."
3. "They've never run anything. They've never made payroll."
4. "They'll tell me to fire people I've worked with for twelve years."
5. "I don't have time for this. I barely have time to read this page."

**What makes them trust.**
1. A fixed fee and a scope in writing before anything starts.
2. Seeing exactly what happens in the first meeting, and what they walk away with.
3. Plain words: "cash" not "liquidity", "who does what" not "org design".
4. Someone who has sat in a back office like theirs (a process, a worksheet, not a claim).
5. Being able to stop after any stage and keep what was written.

**What makes them leave in 5 seconds.** A stock handshake, a glass tower, "unlock your potential", no price
signal at all, a form that asks for annual revenue on step one, or a site that looks built for a Fortune 500 firm.

## 2. The job

**Planned vs urgent.** Almost all planned (around 80%). The urgent 20% is cash: a payroll crunch, a lost big
customer, a lender asking for numbers by Friday. Those owners call; the planned ones book a first conversation.

**Seasonality.**
- January to March: the busiest months. New-year resolutions, tax-time surprises, pricing reviews before spring.
- April to June: hiring plans for seasonal businesses, growth plans, bank and lease conversations.
- July and August: slow; owners are inside their own busy season (landscapers, contractors, ice cream, tourism).
- September to November: budget and planning for next year, succession talks after summer, college-town
  businesses reset when students return (Northampton), defense-contract cycles close (Dayton, Frederick).
- December: quiet except year-end cash planning and "I want next year to be different" calls.

**Average ticket bands (words).** A single working session is a small ticket. A focused project (cash flow plan,
pricing review, hiring plan) is a mid ticket. A full engagement (business plan, growth plan, succession plan over
several months) is a large ticket. Monthly check-ins are a small recurring ticket.

**Services that sell (from facts).** Business strategy, cash flow planning, pricing strategy, hiring and team
structure, operations and systems, marketing plans, sales process, financial reporting, business plans, growth
planning, exit and succession planning, one-on-one coaching.

**Owner words vs consultant words.**
| Owner says | Consultant says |
|---|---|
| "We're busy but there's never any money" | working-capital gap / cash conversion cycle |
| "I can't take a week off" | key-person dependency |
| "I don't know what to charge" | pricing strategy / margin analysis |
| "Nobody knows who does what" | role clarity / org structure |
| "We do everything the hard way" | process documentation / SOPs |
| "The numbers come too late to help" | management reporting cadence |
| "I need something to show the bank" | business plan / financial projections |
| "Who takes over when I'm done?" | succession planning |
| "Should I sell?" | exit planning / transition options |
| "I'm the bottleneck" | delegation / decision rights |
| "We keep hiring the wrong people" | hiring process / role scorecards |
| "Customers say we're cheap" | value positioning / price anchoring |
| "Sales are all word of mouth" | lead pipeline / sales process |

1. The stock handshake across a glass desk, or a team in suits laughing at a laptop.
2. "Unlock your potential" / "strategic solutions for growth" with no verb a baker would use.
3. A lightbulb, a chess piece, a mountain summit or a rocket as the hero image.
4. Logos of past clients and "we helped X grow 300%" (we cannot, and the owner does not believe it).
5. No price anywhere; "contact us for a custom proposal".
6. A contact form asking for annual revenue and number of employees before a name.
7. Five-paragraph founder bios full of certifications.
8. A blog link wall of "10 tips" posts.

## 5. Niche tools (pick from, each site uses 2+)

1. **Cash runway check**
   - Owner learns: how many months the business could cover its fixed costs from cash on hand.
   - Inputs: cash in the bank, monthly fixed costs (payroll, rent, loans), average days customers take to pay.
   - Output: runway in months on a bar, a plain sentence ("About 2.5 months; customers paying in 45 days is the
     biggest lever"), and the matching service with its sample range. Computed in the browser, nothing sent.
   - Leads to the call: "Walk me through my runway."
2. **Price gap worksheet**
   - Owner learns: whether their price covers their real hourly cost.
   - Inputs: what they charge per job or hour, hours a job really takes, materials, overhead per month, billable
     hours per month.
   - Output: true cost per job vs price, the gap as a percent, and "Pricing review" with its sample range.
   - Leads to the call: "Check my prices."
3. **Owner-hours audit**
   - Owner learns: how much of their week only they can do.
   - Inputs: tick from a list of 14 weekly tasks (payroll, quoting, ordering, hiring, scheduling...), who else could
     do it (nobody / someone with training / someone now).
   - Output: a week drawn as blocks, the hours that could move, and "Hiring & team structure" or "Operations".
   - Leads to the call: "Get my week back."
4. **Where to start quiz**
   - Owner learns: which one service fits their problem first.
   - Inputs: 4 questions in owner words ("money", "people", "prices", "what's next").
   - Output: one service, why, the first meeting's agenda, the sample range.
   - Leads to the call: "Book that first meeting."
5. **Engagement planner**
   - Owner learns: what each week of a project looks like and what they owe us (documents, hours).
   - Inputs: the service, how many hours a week they can give.
   - Output: a week-by-week calendar with meetings, homework and deliverables.
   - Leads to the call: "Hold these dates."
6. **Succession timeline**
   - Owner learns: how long a handover usually takes and the order of steps.
   - Inputs: their age band, who might take over (family / manager / outside buyer / not sure), target year.
   - Output: a drawn timeline from now to the target year with milestones (numbers in order, documented jobs,
     successor training, legal and tax advisors brought in), and the sample range for a succession plan.
   - Leads to the call: "Start the timeline."
7. **Reporting checklist**
   - Owner learns: which five numbers they should see every month and which they already get.
   - Inputs: tick what they receive today.
   - Output: a one-page monthly report preview with the missing lines highlighted.
   - Leads to the call: "Set up my monthly numbers."
8. **ZIP checker** (all three sites): served in person / by video, from `zips`.
9. **Multi-step request form** (all three sites): what's going on (owner words); business type and size band
   (never revenue first); how urgent; best time; contact.

## 6. The three directions

### A — Six Thursdays, after you close   (axis: PROCESS — how the work is done, shown step by step)
- Brand: Tenpenny Business Advisory · Northampton, MA · facts-A.json
- Concept seed: The whole engagement is six working sessions held on Thursday evenings, after the owner's shop
  closes, at the owner's own table. The page walks the six sessions in order: what we bring, what you bring, what
  you leave with, and the one piece of homework. A Pioneer Valley owner (a Main Street bookshop, a Florence
  bakery, a Hadley farm stand that went year-round) sees the whole thing before saying yes.
- Tone lead: dark-led (after-hours: deep ink-blue near-black, warm desk-lamp cream for paper, one brick-red
  accent from the old mill buildings).
- Type lead: serif (a literary text serif with good italics and old-style figures for the session headings and the
  owner's quotes; a quiet humanist sans for UI and forms).
- Hero family: 12 sticky photo with scrolling text (a lamp-lit table with an open notebook, a mug and a calculator
  stays pinned while three lines scroll past: "Thursday, 6:30. The shop is closed." / "Six sessions. One plan."
  / "You leave each one with a page you can use Friday." The first line is visible without scrolling.)
- Signature seed: the session ledger. Each section is one Thursday, with a hand-set session number ("Thursday 3
  of 6"), a margin column of "you bring / you leave with", and a homework slip that tears off at the end. The
  six slips collect at the bottom of the page into the finished plan's table of contents.
- Motion idea: intro: the lamp warms up (a radial light fades in on the photo over 0.8 s) before the first line
  sets. Scroll story: the session counter advances 1 to 6 down the page and each homework slip slides into the
  "plan" stack at the foot of the page.
- Tools: engagement planner, where-to-start quiz, ZIP checker, multi-step request form.
- Photo mood: evening, warm lamp light, close and quiet; tables, notebooks, hands, the inside of a closed shop
  with chairs up; brick and maples outside in fall. Never a boardroom.

- Photo mood: daylight, clean and close: hands on a calculator, printed ledgers, a laptop spreadsheet with no
